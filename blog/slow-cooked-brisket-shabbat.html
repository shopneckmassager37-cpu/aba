The Secret to Slow-Cooked Brisket

Published July 2026 · 5 min read

The Chefaleh Kitchen · notes from behind the counter

Brisket is a forgiving cut and an unforgiving test at the same time. Forgiving, because it's built to withstand hours of heat. Unforgiving, because there is nowhere to hide a rushed process — you either gave it the time it needed, or you didn't, and the texture on the plate will say so.

At Chefaleh, brisket is the centerpiece of more Shabbat tables than any other dish we make. Here's what actually matters when you're cooking it at home.

1. Low and slow isn't a slogan — it's chemistry

Brisket is full of collagen, the connective tissue that makes cheap cuts tough. Collagen doesn't soften by getting hot; it softens by staying hot for a long time, breaking down into gelatin somewhere between 190°F and 205°F internal temperature. Cook it fast at high heat and you'll hit that internal temperature before the collagen has had a chance to convert — the result is meat that's technically "done" but still chewy. We cook ours low, covered, for a long stretch — think hours, not minutes — until a fork slides in with almost no resistance.

2. Cut against the grain, always

Even a perfectly cooked brisket can taste tough if it's sliced the wrong way. Look at the muscle fibers running through the meat and cut perpendicular to them, not parallel. This shortens the fibers on your fork, which is most of what "tenderness" actually is on the palate. It's a 10-second decision that changes the entire eating experience.

3. Let it rest — then let it rest again

Pulling brisket straight from the oven and slicing it immediately lets all the rendered juice run out onto the cutting board instead of back into the meat. We rest ours, loosely tented, for at least 20–30 minutes before the first cut. If you're making it ahead for Friday (which we recommend), let it cool, slice it once fully rested, then reheat gently in its own liquid — the second rest, in the braising liquid, is often what makes a make-ahead brisket taste even better than one served the same day.

None of this is complicated. It's mostly about resisting the urge to rush a cut of meat that was never designed to be rushed.
