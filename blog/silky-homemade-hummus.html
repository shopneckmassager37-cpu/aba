Silky Homemade Hummus, No Shortcuts

Published July 2026 · 5 min read

Most homemade hummus tastes fine and feels wrong — grainy, heavy, more like a paste than the cloud-like dip you get at a proper Middle Eastern kitchen. The gap almost always comes down to three choices made long before the food processor gets involved.

1. Start dried, not canned

Canned chickpeas are convenient, but their skins are tougher and the beans themselves never get quite as soft as chickpeas you've cooked yourself from dried. Soak dried chickpeas overnight, then cook them low and slow until they're genuinely falling-apart soft — softer than you'd want for a salad, closer to the texture of a well-cooked bean for soup. Undercooked chickpeas are the single biggest reason home hummus turns out grainy.

2. Add baking soda to the cooking water

A small amount of baking soda in the cooking water raises the pH, which breaks down the pectin holding the chickpea skins together. The skins loosen and start to slip off in the pot, and what's left cooks down into a much smoother base. It's a small step that makes a bigger textural difference than any amount of extra blending.

3. Blend the tahini and lemon first — without the chickpeas

This is the trick most home cooks skip. Blend tahini, lemon juice, garlic and ice water together on their own until the mixture turns pale, fluffy and noticeably lighter in color — this can take a few minutes and looks almost like it's failing right up until it isn't. Only then add the warm, well-cooked chickpeas and blend again. Building the emulsion first, before the chickpeas go in, is what gives finished hummus that whipped, almost mousse-like texture instead of a dense purée.

Dried beans, a pinch of baking soda, and patience with the tahini — none of it is difficult, it just takes longer than the shortcut version most recipes promise.
